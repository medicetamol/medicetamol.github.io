# Retrobulbar Neuritis

**Retrobulbar (optic) neuritis** causes sudden, often painful (worse with eye movement) monocular vision loss with a **relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD)**, but because the inflammation affects the optic nerve **behind** the globe, the optic disc appears **normal** on fundoscopy at presentation ("the patient sees nothing, and the doctor sees nothing").

## Trigger Point

> **Sudden painful vision loss + RAPD + normal-appearing disc**: the classic description of retrobulbar neuritis.

## Why Not the Other Options?

**A. Anterior Ischaemic Optic Neuropathy (AION)**
Causes sudden, typically **painless** vision loss with a **swollen, pale disc** on fundoscopy (the inflammation/infarction is at the disc itself, anterior to the retrobulbar segment).
The disc is abnormal in AION, unlike the normal disc described here.

**B. Central Retinal Artery Occlusion**
Causes sudden, painless vision loss with a **pale retina and a cherry-red spot** at the macula, a very different fundoscopic picture.
Not associated with pain, and the fundus is far from normal-appearing.

**D. Papilledema**
Reflects raised intracranial pressure, and characteristically shows a **swollen optic disc bilaterally**, usually without acute severe vision loss early on.
The disc is markedly abnormal, unlike this case.

## Mind Capsule

> Painful vision loss + RAPD + normal disc = retrobulbar neuritis ("nothing to see, but the patient sees nothing").
