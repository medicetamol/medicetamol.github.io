# Both Assertion and Reason Are Correct, But They Are Unrelated

A **nested case-control study** is conducted within an existing, defined **cohort**, so because the entire source population (denominator) is known, **incidence rates can indeed be derived** from the parent cohort — the assertion is true. Separately, nested case-control studies **can** estimate relative risk (or an approximation of it) accurately, often more efficiently than the full cohort analysis — the reason is also true. However, these are two **independent** advantages of the design, not a cause-and-effect relationship: the ability to calculate incidence does not explain why relative risk estimation is accurate.

## Trigger Point

> **Both statements about nested case-control studies are true**, but one does not explain the other.

## Why Not the Other Options?

**A. Both Correct, Reason Explains Assertion**
The reason (accurate relative risk estimation) does not causally explain the assertion (ability to derive incidence from the parent cohort); they are two separate, independently true features of the design.
Treating one as the explanation for the other overstates the connection.

**C. Assertion Correct, Reason Incorrect**
The reason is also genuinely true: nested case-control studies, embedded in a well-defined cohort, do provide reliable relative risk estimates.
Both statements, not just the assertion, are correct.

**D. Assertion Incorrect, Reason Correct**
The assertion is also true: knowing the full parent cohort does allow incidence rates to be calculated, which is precisely the design's advantage over a standalone case-control study.
Both statements, not just the reason, are correct.

## Mind Capsule

> Nested case-control studies: parent-cohort denominator allows incidence calculation AND accurate relative risk estimation — two separate strengths, not cause and effect.
