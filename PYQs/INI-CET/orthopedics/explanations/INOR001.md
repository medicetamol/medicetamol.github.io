# Nailing the Femurs, External Fixation of the Tibiae

In a polytrauma patient with chest injury and haemodynamic instability, the principle of **"damage control orthopaedics"** applies: fractures are initially managed with rapid, low-physiological-cost **external fixation**, and definitive fixation is delayed until the patient is stable. Once resuscitated and stabilised, **intramedullary nailing of the femurs** (a major long bone, best treated with nailing) is combined with **external fixation of the tibiae**, reflecting standard damage-control-to-definitive staged management as described in this scenario.

## Key Finding

> X-ray shows a long-bone fracture, consistent with the described polytrauma injuries.

## Trigger Point

> **Chest trauma + haemodynamic instability + bilateral femur and tibia fractures**, now stabilised: definitive fixation strategy.

## Why Not the Other Options?

**B. Plating of Tibiae and External Fixation of Femurs**
Reverses the standard approach; femoral shaft fractures are preferentially treated with intramedullary nailing, not external fixation, once the patient is stable.
This combination does not match damage-control-to-definitive staging principles.

**C. Whichever Procedure the Surgeon Can Perform Fastest**
This describes damage control done during the unstable phase, not the definitive management once the patient is stabilised.
The question specifically asks about the stage after stabilisation.

**D. External Fixation of Both Femurs and Both Tibiae**
Prolonged external fixation of femurs is not the definitive treatment of choice once a patient is stable; definitive nailing is preferred for the femur.
This is an initial damage-control measure, not the final approach.

## Mind Capsule

> Damage control (unstable) = whatever is fastest; once stabilised = definitive fixation (femur nailing preferred).
