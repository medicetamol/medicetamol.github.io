# Emergency Fasciotomy

A **compartment pressure around 30 to 40 mmHg or higher** (and especially a pressure as high as 90 mmHg), combined with **pain out of proportion to injury, unrelieved by analgesics, and an absent distal pulse**, indicates established **acute compartment syndrome** after trauma. This is a surgical emergency requiring **immediate fasciotomy** to prevent irreversible muscle and nerve ischaemia.

## Trigger Point

> **Post-RTA + uncontrolled pain + compartment pressure 90 mmHg + absent pulse**: compartment syndrome requiring emergency fasciotomy.

## Why Not the Other Options?

**B. Observation**
Delaying treatment in established compartment syndrome risks irreversible muscle necrosis and nerve damage within hours.
Not appropriate once the diagnosis is clinically and pressure-confirmed.

**C. Increase IV Analgesics**
Pain here is refractory to analgesics precisely because it is from ischaemia, not simple nociceptive pain; escalating analgesia does not treat the underlying compartment pressure.
Masks the problem rather than treating it, and risks delaying definitive care.

**D. External Fixation**
Addresses fracture stability, not the elevated compartment pressure itself.
Does not relieve the ischaemia causing tissue damage.

## Mind Capsule

> Compartment syndrome: pain out of proportion + high compartment pressure + pulselessness = emergency fasciotomy, no delay.
